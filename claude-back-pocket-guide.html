The Claude Glossary

Search, or click any term to expand it. Each entry has what it is, when you'd actually use it, anything to watch out for, and a quick example.

Workspaces and organisation

Projects

a dedicated workspace where related chats, files, and instructions live together

What it is: A dedicated workspace inside Claude where you store instructions, upload files, and keep related chats together. Everything inside a Project shares the same context, so Claude doesn't forget what you're working on every time you open a new chat.

When you'd use it: Anywhere you do the same kind of work repeatedly (client comms, content planning, admin for one area of your business). Set up the Project once with your voice, your files, your context, and every new chat inside it starts with all of that already loaded.

Heads up: There are two things called "Projects" in Claude. The one most people mean is claude.ai Projects (browser-based, works on phone and desktop). The other is Cowork Projects, which live on your actual computer (desktop only, paid plans). If someone says "put it in a Project" they almost always mean the browser version.

Example: A Project called "Client emails" with your tone guidelines, a few sample replies you like, and a note about how formal you want to be. Every time you paste a new message to respond to, Claude already knows how you write.

Knowledge Base

the files you upload into a Project for Claude to reference

What it is: The files you upload into a Project so Claude can reference them. Your service menu, your brand guidelines, a client brief, a transcript, anything Claude might need to look at regularly.

When you'd use it: When you have information that doesn't change much but Claude needs to know. Rather than pasting it into every chat, you put it in the Project's knowledge base and Claude can access it automatically whenever it's relevant.

Heads up: "Knowledge base" isn't always a button labelled that way. In a claude.ai Project it's usually just the section where you add files and instructions. The term comes up a lot in the AI world, so worth knowing what people mean.

Example: Your "Client Onboarding" Project has your welcome document, your intake form template, and your FAQs uploaded. When a new enquiry comes in, you can say "draft a response using my intake form language" and Claude has it.

System Prompt

the standing instructions that tell Claude how to behave before you say anything

What it is: A set of standing instructions that tells Claude how to behave, before your actual message. It's the "always do this, never do that" layer. In a claude.ai Project, this is the "custom instructions" or "project instructions" field.

When you'd use it: Whenever you find yourself giving Claude the same ground rules in every chat. Tone, voice, what to avoid, what format to default to. Put it in the instructions once and stop repeating yourself.

Example: Project instructions that say: "Australian English. Warm and practical, no corporate or coaching language. Ask clarifying questions before writing long pieces. Don't invent client quotes or stats." That's a system prompt, working in the background of every chat in that Project.

Memory

Claude remembering things about you across different conversations

What it is: Claude remembering things about you and your work across conversations, so you don't have to reintroduce yourself every time. You can see what it's remembered, edit it, and turn it off if you prefer.

When you'd use it: It's on by default for most users. Helpful for the basics: your business, your tone, recurring projects. You can also ask Claude directly: "remember that I prefer short replies" or "forget what you know about my old business name."

Heads up: Memory is different from a Project. A Project is a workspace you organise with intent. Memory is background continuity Claude maintains on its own. They work well together, but they're not the same thing.

Example: You start a new chat and ask "help me with a LinkedIn post." Claude already knows you run an AI education business, prefer Australian English, and don't want corporate language, because it remembers from previous chats.
Features and actions

Skills

reusable instructions Claude applies automatically when they're relevant

What it is: A reusable set of instructions Claude can pull up automatically when it's relevant. Think of it as a small instruction manual you write once. Claude checks if it applies to what you're doing and uses it if it does.

Two kinds: The ones Anthropic builds in (Excel, Word, PowerPoint, PDF) work behind the scenes whenever you ask for those file types. Custom ones you write yourself or upload as a zip are where the real leverage is.

When you'd use it: When you have a specific way you want something done every time (brand guidelines, a content format you use weekly, the exact structure of your invoices). Write the skill once and Claude will reach for it whenever you're doing that kind of work, without you having to re-explain.

Example: A skill called "my-brand-voice" that describes how you write (warm, no jargon, Australian English, no em-dashes). Any time you ask Claude to draft something, it applies the skill automatically.

Plugins

bundled packs of skills, usually built for a specific role or task

What it is: A pre-built bundle of skills (and sometimes commands) that install together, usually focused on a specific job or role. Think "Sales plugin" or "Marketing plugin". Installing a plugin gives you a whole set of related abilities at once, rather than building each skill yourself.

When you'd use it: When someone else has already put together what you need. If you're new to Claude, browsing plugins can be a fast way to see what's possible for your line of work before you start writing your own skills.

Heads up: Plugins are mainly browsed and installed through the desktop app (inside Cowork). Treat them like apps you install: have a look at what they do before you enable them, and turn off anything you're not actually using.

Example: You install a "Marketing" plugin and suddenly have skills for brief writing, competitor research, and social copy, without setting any of them up yourself.

Connectors

official links between Claude and apps like Gmail, Calendar, Drive, Notion

What it is: Official links between Claude and other apps you already use (Gmail, Google Calendar, Google Drive, Notion, Canva, and more). Once you connect an app, Claude can read from it, and sometimes write to it, without you copying and pasting.

When you'd use it: Any time you catch yourself pasting the same kind of content into Claude repeatedly (your calendar for the week, a document from Drive, an email thread). Connect the app once and Claude can just reach for it.

Heads up: You control what Claude can access. Connecting Gmail doesn't mean Claude reads every email forever, it means Claude can look when you ask it to, with your permission. You can disconnect any connector at any time.

Example: With the Google Calendar connector on, you can ask "what's on my schedule tomorrow and what do I need to prep for?" and Claude pulls the events and gives you a briefing.

Artifacts

content Claude produces as a standalone, editable thing in its own panel

What it is: Content Claude produces as a standalone, editable thing in its own panel, rather than just text inside the chat. Documents, code, diagrams, little web apps, tables. You can iterate on them, download them, or keep working on them across the conversation.

When you'd use it: Automatically, most of the time. Claude makes an artifact when it's generating something longer or more structured than a chat reply. Worth knowing the word so you can say "can you put that in an artifact" or "update the artifact with..." instead of scrolling for it.

Example: You ask Claude to write a welcome email sequence. It produces the sequence as an artifact on the right-hand side. You say "make email two warmer" and it edits that artifact directly, so you don't get four full rewrites in the chat.

Cowork

Claude running on your actual computer, able to work on real files

What it is: Claude, but running on your actual computer instead of in a browser tab. It can read and write files in folders you give it access to, produce finished documents, and run multi-step tasks on its own. Launched as a research preview in January 2026 and became generally available on 9 April 2026 for paid plans.

When you'd use it: When "have a conversation with Claude" isn't what you need. When you want Claude to go away, do the work on real files, and come back with something finished. Good for recurring admin, producing deliverables, working across multiple files at once.

Heads up: Cowork is desktop only (Mac and Windows) and paid plans only (Pro, Max, Team, Enterprise). If you're on the free plan or on your phone, you won't see it. It also needs the desktop app to be open and your computer awake to run scheduled work.

Example: "Go through my /Invoices folder, find anything unpaid more than 30 days old, and draft follow-up emails in a new folder." Claude does the scan, writes the drafts, saves them. You review and send.

Scheduled Tasks

tasks Claude runs automatically on a schedule, inside Cowork

What it is: Tasks you set up once and Claude runs automatically on a schedule (daily, weekly, on weekdays), or that you can trigger on demand. They live inside Cowork and can use everything Cowork can: your files, connectors, skills.

When you'd use it: Anything repetitive with a predictable rhythm. A Monday-morning brief that pulls your calendar and top emails. A weekly content round-up. A monthly client report. Set it once, and it runs without you.

Heads up: Scheduled tasks are Cowork only, so desktop and paid plans. Your computer has to be on and the Claude app running for them to fire. Closing the app pauses everything.

Example: "Every Monday at 7am, check my calendar for the week, summarise any overdue invoices from my Invoices folder, and save it as a markdown file called week-brief.md." You open the file with your coffee.

Dispatch

triggering a Cowork task from your phone while it runs on your desktop

What it is: A way to start a Cowork task from your phone (via the Claude mobile app) even though the work itself runs on your desktop computer. You send a message, Cowork picks it up on your Mac or PC, does the task, and the result is waiting for you when you're back at your desk.

When you'd use it: When you're away from your computer but think of something you want Claude to do. Commuting, in a meeting, waiting at school pickup. "While I'm out, pull together a draft social caption from yesterday's blog and save it to my Drafts folder."

Heads up: For Dispatch to work, your desktop has to be on and the Claude app running. It's not magic from the cloud, it's your own computer doing the work remotely.

Example: On the train: you message Claude "prep a summary of the Smith client file and draft three possible email replies." By the time you're home, the file is in your folder ready to review.
Under the hood

Context Window

how much Claude can hold in its head at once during a conversation

What it is: How much Claude can hold in its head at once during a conversation. Everything you've said, everything it's replied, plus any files you've attached. It's measured in tokens (roughly, words). The current top Claude models can hold around a million tokens, which is hundreds of pages of text.

When it matters: In very long chats or with very large documents. Claude can start to lose track of things from early in the conversation once the window fills up. If Claude suddenly feels forgetful or inconsistent, the context window is a likely suspect.

What to do about it: Start a new chat for a new topic rather than stretching one conversation across everything. If the thread is getting long, ask Claude to summarise where you've got to, then carry that summary into a fresh chat.

Example: You've been working on a content plan in one chat for two hours and Claude starts contradicting things from earlier. Start a new chat, paste in the plan so far, and keep going. Fresh context, clean head.

Model Differences

Opus, Sonnet, Haiku: different sizes of Claude for different jobs

What it is: Claude isn't one model, it's a family of them at different sizes. Opus is the smartest and slowest, Sonnet is the everyday workhorse, Haiku is the fastest for simple stuff. As of April 2026 the current versions are Opus 4.7, Sonnet 4.6, and Haiku 4.5.

When to use which: Sonnet is the default and handles the vast majority of real work (writing, analysis, planning, drafting). Reach for Opus when the task is genuinely complex (long documents, tricky reasoning, layered decisions). Haiku is for quick, high-volume tasks where speed matters more than depth.

Heads up: Free accounts get a limited version. Paid plans let you switch between models. If you're on Pro and nothing feels wrong, you're probably on Sonnet, which is correct for most of what you're doing.

Example: Drafting a newsletter: Sonnet. Reviewing a 40-page council tender and pulling out what's relevant: Opus. Sorting a list of 200 feedback comments into themes: Haiku, because it's fast and cheap at scale.

Claude Code

Claude for developers, runs in the terminal, probably not for you

What it is: A version of Claude built for software developers. It runs in the terminal (a text-based app on your computer) and is designed to work inside code projects: reading files, writing code, running commands, managing version control. Completely separate product from Cowork, claude.ai, and the mobile app.

When you'd use it: Honestly, probably never. If you're running a small business and you're not writing software, this isn't for you. The reason it's in the glossary: you'll hear people online raving about it, and now you know what they mean and can cheerfully set it aside.

Heads up: Some non-developers do use Claude Code for heavy file work (batch-renaming hundreds of files, reorganising a large project folder, working across many documents at once). If that's you, it's worth a look. For everyone else, Cowork does most of the same jobs without the terminal.

Example of who this is for: A web developer building a client website. They open the terminal in their code project and say "add a contact form to the about page and update the navigation." Claude edits the files directly.

Section two

Where to find it in Claude

Knowing what something is and knowing where to click are two different problems. This is a rough orientation. The menus do move around occasionally, so treat it as a starting point.

Projects (claude.ai)

Left sidebar in the browser or app, look for "Projects" above your chat history. "New Project" button at the top.

Skills

Customize (in the left sidebar) → Skills. Toggle existing skills on or off, or click the "+" to upload a custom one.

Connectors

Customize → Connectors. Browse available apps, click to connect, confirm the permissions. Disconnect the same way.

Plugins

In the desktop app: Customize → Personal plugins. Click the "+" to browse and install. Each plugin bundles a set of skills.

Memory

Settings → Profile (or Personalization). You'll see what Claude remembers, can edit entries, or turn memory off.

Cowork

Only in the desktop app, on Pro / Max / Team / Enterprise plans. If it's not visible, you're either on free or in the browser.

Scheduled Tasks

Inside Cowork → "Scheduled" in the left sidebar. Or type /schedule inside any Cowork task.

Dispatch

From the Claude mobile app, send a message to Cowork. It runs on your desktop and the result shows up in the same thread.

Model selector

Dropdown at the top of the chat window, usually next to or above the message box. Shows the current model, tap to change.

Artifacts

Appear automatically on the right-hand side of the chat when Claude produces them. Nothing to turn on.

Claude Code

Separate product. Install through your terminal (type claude to launch). Not in the main Claude app at all.

Section three

When Claude isn't getting it

Everyone hits these moments. Here's what's usually going on, and the fastest way to unstick it.

Claude's giving me something that sounds right but isn't.

What's happening

Claude is trained to sound fluent, which can mean confidently generating something that isn't true: invented statistics, quotes that don't exist, a client testimonial it pulled from nowhere. This is sometimes called "hallucination."

What to do

Ask it to show its source, or tell it explicitly what it is and isn't allowed to invent. For anything you'd actually publish or send, check the specifics yourself: names, numbers, dates, direct quotes. Don't ask Claude to make up case studies, stats, or testimonials you can't verify.

It keeps giving me generic, AI-sounding copy.

What's happening

Claude defaults to a safe, polished register when it doesn't have anything better to work from. Without samples of your actual voice, it reaches for whatever was most common in its training, which is corporate and predictable.

What to do

Give it something real to work from. Paste in two or three examples of how you actually write. Tell it what to avoid as well as what to aim for ("no metaphors about journeys, no words like woven or unlock"). The more specific the negative guidance, the less it defaults to AI-voice.

It's ignoring something I told it three messages ago.

What's happening

Long chats stretch Claude's attention. The further back an instruction is, the more likely it'll drift. This isn't laziness, it's the context window filling up.

What to do

Re-anchor. State the key rule again ("remember: Australian English, no em-dashes") at the point it matters. For anything you want Claude to respect every time, put it in the Project instructions rather than burying it in a chat.

It's asking me question after question instead of doing the thing.

What's happening

Claude is trying to be careful and not guess wrong. Useful in the right context, annoying when you just want it to get on with it.

What to do

Tell it to make reasonable assumptions and flag them as it goes: "Make sensible assumptions where you have to, just note them at the end so I can correct any that are off." That gives Claude permission to move, and you the chance to adjust.

The output's technically correct but not what I wanted.

What's happening

Usually the brief was ambiguous. Claude interpreted it one reasonable way, and you meant a different reasonable way.

What to do

Don't rewrite from scratch. Say what's wrong and what you want instead: "This is too formal and too long. Keep the structure, cut it in half, make it sound like it could be said out loud." Claude adjusts the existing draft rather than starting over, and you preserve the bits that worked.

It suddenly got worse mid-conversation.

What's happening

Either the chat is very long and the context is overloaded, or you've piled up so many competing instructions that Claude can't prioritise. Sometimes it's also the wrong model for the task.

What to do

Start a fresh chat. Paste in the last good output and the remaining task, and keep going. If the task is genuinely heavy (long document, layered reasoning), switch to Opus for that bit.

Section four

Prompting basics worth knowing

You've probably seen the usual list: be clear, give context, specify format. All fine, all true, all in every AI article on the internet. These are the moves that actually change the quality of what you get back.

01

Show, don't describe.

Telling Claude "write in a warm, conversational tone" is vague. Warm to whom? Claude fills the gap with its best guess.

Instead, paste in two or three examples of writing you love and say "match this." One good example beats three paragraphs of description.

02

Name what you don't want.

Most advice tells you to describe what you do want. That's half the work.

The other half is the prohibitions: "no words like woven, unlock, journey, dive. No rhetorical questions. No em-dashes. No CTA at the end." Specifics shake loose the AI-voice.

03

Ask for thinking before output.

For anything with real stakes, ask Claude to think it through first before drafting.

"Before you write this, talk me through the key considerations and what you'd recommend." Slows Claude down, surfaces better decisions, and gives you the chance to steer.

04

Give it a role that earns its keep.

"Act as a copywriter" on its own is too generic.

What works is role plus the perspective that role brings: "Act as an editor reviewing this for a small-business audience. Where am I losing them? What would you cut?" The role sets up the questions, that's where the useful feedback comes from.

05

Iterate, don't restart.

When something's 70% right, the instinct is to re-prompt with a bigger version of the original brief. Usually throws away the good bits.

Instead: "Keep the opening. The middle is too generic, make it more specific and cut the metaphor. The ending is fine." Two or three rounds, not eight.

06

Put the ask first, context second.

Long prompts often open with paragraphs of background before the actual request. Claude reads everything, but the thing closest to where it starts writing gets the most weight.

Lead with the ask: "I need a 150-word LinkedIn post about X. Context below." Small change, noticeable difference on longer prompts.
